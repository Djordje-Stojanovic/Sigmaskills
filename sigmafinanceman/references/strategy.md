# One method for any investment

Read this for every question that puts money at risk: a stock, a fund, a trade, a startup cheque, a flat, a coin, a GPU, a stock of goods, a car, a whole business. The method has nine steps. Run them in order. Then read the playbook for the kind of investment at hand.

The method merges the fourteen people in [masters.md](masters.md). Tags such as `Buffett P1` point to a principle there. Steps and defaults below are ours. A default is a starting value. The user's number overrides it.

## What the reply must show

1. The verdict first: buy, pass, size or sell, in one sentence.
2. The thesis, the edge, and what would prove the thesis wrong.
3. The numbers from steps 3 and 4, with their sources. A number nobody gave you is an assumption: mark it.
4. The worst case in money, and the size that keeps it survivable.
5. The principles applied, by tag, and any disagreement the step had to settle.
6. The sell rule, written before the buy.

## The nine steps

### 1. Thesis and edge

Write the thesis in two sentences: what you own, and why it is worth more than you pay. Then write the edge: why you see this and the seller does not. An edge is one of four things: you know the business better (Buffett P4), you see the future before the price does (Druckenmiller P4, Thiel P5), you have cheaper capital or a longer horizon (Ackman P5, Buffett P2), or you have access others lack.

No edge means the default is the low-cost index fund. Buffett told the non-professional to "own a cross-section of businesses" through "a low-cost S&P 500 index fund" (Berkshire letter, 2013), and told his trustee to put 90% there. Then argue the other side: find the smartest person who disagrees and listen to the whole case (Ackman P4). Completion test: the thesis fits in two sentences, the edge names one of the four, and the best opposing case is written down.

### 2. Downside first

Ask what you lose before what you win. Write three cases: base, bad, and ruin. Ruin is the case that ends you: a margin call, a lender pulling a credit line (Musk P5), a forced sale at the bottom. Munger's rule is to avoid debt, and Buffett's is that leverage "produces zeroes" (Buffett P5). Druckenmiller was right on a market and still lost, because of leverage (Druckenmiller P5).

Test every idea for four ways to lose: the business is bad, the price is too high, the funding breaks, the timing is wrong. If ruin is possible, redesign the funding or pass. Completion test: the worst case is a number in money and a percentage of your capital.

### 3. The numbers

Use the few numbers that decide the answer. Show each with its formula.

- **Cash flow.** What the asset pays you after every cost. For a business, use owner earnings: reported earnings plus depreciation and other non-cash charges, minus the average capital spending needed to hold its position (Buffett, Berkshire letter 1986). Thiel says a business is worth the sum of all the money it will make (Thiel P3).
- **Unit economics.** What one unit earns: one customer, one flat, one GPU-hour, one car. Revenue per unit minus the cost to serve it. If one unit loses money, scale makes it worse.
- **Payback.** All-in cost divided by net cash per period. Leone draws the line at two years for spending that buys growth (Leone P3).
- **Return against a hurdle.** Compute the internal rate of return (IRR) on the cash flows, after tax and fees. The hurdle is what the index offers, plus a premium for illiquidity and your effort. If the IRR does not beat the hurdle with room to spare, pass.
- **Margin of safety.** 1 minus price divided by your value estimate. Default: want at least 25%. Raise it when the estimate is shaky; lower it for great businesses at fair prices (Buffett P1, Ackman P2).
- **Reverse test.** Say what the price already assumes (growth, margin, occupancy, utilisation, resale). If you cannot say it, you cannot judge it.

Stress each result: cut revenue by a fifth, cut the price you sell at by a quarter, add a year to the payback. The idea must survive the stress or be sized for its failure.

### 4. Size the bet

Sizing decides the result more than picking does. Set a loss budget per idea, then size so that the worst case stays inside it.

Position size = loss budget / loss in the bad case. Ackman accepts losing 5-6% of capital in one investment (Ackman P3). If the bad case loses 40% of the position, then 5% / 40% gives a position of 12.5% of capital. If the bad case is a total loss, the position is 5%.

Concentration needs a reason you can state. Druckenmiller and Thiel back few bets (Druckenmiller P1, Thiel P2). Buffett and Munger wait for the rare mispriced bet and then load up (Munger P2). All of them size by how sure they are and how much they can lose, not by how much they want. Diversify when you lack an edge, when you cannot tell the winners in advance, or when the downside of any one holding is ruin.

### 5. Funding, liquidity and leverage

Match the money to the holding period (Ackman P5). Money you may need within two years does not go into a five-year illiquid asset. Keep cash for emergencies before you invest (Buffett P6): cash is the thing nobody notices until it is gone.

Borrow only on terms that cannot force a sale: no margin call, no short maturity against a slow asset, a payment the asset covers under stress. Check the lender's right to cancel (Musk P5). A debt payment larger than the stress-case cash flow is a bet on the future, not an investment.

### 6. Entry price

Pay a price that leaves margin. A great business at a fair price beats a fair business at a great price (Buffett P1). A great business can still be a bad buy when the price assumes everything goes right. In a rising market, say so: Leone says "We are in a bubble," and that picking the right companies still pays at a high price (Leone, ElevenLabs Summit London 2026). Both halves matter: the warning and the condition.

For an uncertain entry, buy in parts. Plan the parts before the first buy. Do not average down on a broken thesis.

### 7. Hold and add

Hold while the thesis is intact. Buffett's favourite holding period is forever, for outstanding businesses with outstanding managers (Buffett P2). Munger points to the tax gain of sitting still (Munger P3). Time works for quality and against junk.

Add when the thesis gets stronger or the price falls on no change in facts. Huang's test: "The stock price changed, but did something else change?" (Huang P2). Review on a schedule and on news that touches the thesis. Do not review on price alone.

### 8. Sell

Write the sell rule before the buy. Sell for one of these reasons:

1. **The thesis broke.** Facts changed, not the price. Cut fast and do not argue with it (Druckenmiller P5).
2. **A better use of the money exists.** Compare with your best alternative, and with the index.
3. **The price passed your value by a wide margin.** Take profits in parts.
4. **The position grew past its cap.** Trim to the size rule.
5. **The money has a new job.** Cash needed, debt due, or risk to cut. Leone advises founders to take 10-20% off the table to take the pressure off (Leone P1).
6. **The funding is under stress.** Sell the liquid thing before the market forces you to sell it at the bottom.

A fall in price is not a reason. A fall with a broken thesis is.

### 9. Review and the mistakes

After every position closes, write what you expected, what happened, and the lesson. Ackman says they treasure mistakes (Ackman P6). Munger says the most extreme mistakes were mistakes of omission, bets seen and not made (Munger P4). Count both: the bad buys and the good ones you skipped.

The classic mistakes, for every kind:

- Buying a story with no numbers.
- Paying for growth that the price already counted.
- Using debt that can force a sale.
- Putting too much in one idea with no edge, or too little in the one with a strong edge.
- Treating a price drop as a thesis change, or a thesis change as a price drop.
- Ignoring costs, tax and time: fees, spreads, repairs, vacancies, your own hours.
- Selling winners early and holding losers for hope.
- Following a crowd in a bubble, or fighting one without a reason or a limit.
- Skipping the exit: not knowing how to get out and what it costs.

## Where the masters disagree, and how the method decides

**Concentrate or spread.** Druckenmiller calls diversification "the most misguided concept" and says to put eggs in one basket and watch it (Druckenmiller P1). Thiel says life is not a portfolio (Thiel P2). Buffett told the non-professional to hold an index fund (letter 2013), and Leone points out that about ten companies drive each year's returns, so a venture investor must be in those ten (Leone P5). The method: concentrate only with an edge and a loss budget. Without an edge, own the index. In venture, where no one can tell the winners in advance, own enough shots to reach the winners and size each one small.

**Macro and timing or business only.** Druckenmiller says liquidity moves markets and that you invest in the future (Druckenmiller P3, P4). Buffett and Munger value the business and treat market mood only as a price signal (Buffett P3). The method: for a stock, the business decides the buy, and liquidity and rates adjust the size and the pace. For a macro trade, liquidity is the thesis, and the loss budget and cut rule are stricter.

**Hold forever or cut fast.** Buffett holds great businesses for as long as they stay great (Buffett P2). Druckenmiller cuts when the facts change (Druckenmiller P5). They agree on the test: has the thesis changed? Forever applies to a strong thesis, and fast applies to a broken one.

**Leverage.** Druckenmiller says go for the jugular when conviction is high (Druckenmiller P2). Buffett, Munger and Ackman fear debt (Buffett P5, Munger P5, Ackman P6). The method: size up, not borrow up. High conviction raises the position inside the loss budget. It does not add debt that can force a sale.

**Pay up or wait.** Buffett and Ackman want a fair to cheap price. Thiel, Bezos and Leone accept a high price for a company that can become overwhelmingly valuable. The method: pay up only for a durable, growing cash flow, and only after the reverse test shows what the price assumes. The more the price assumes, the smaller the position.

**Dividends or reinvestment.** Buffett's retained earnings compounded into new businesses (Buffett letter 2018). Musk and Bezos pour cash back into the next product (Musk P1, Bezos P1). A dividend is right when the company cannot reinvest at a high return. Retaining is right when it can. Ask for the return on retained cash.

## Playbooks by kind

Each has what to check, the numbers, sizing, when to sell, and the classic mistakes. The nine steps still apply.

### Growth stocks

Check: durable growth, high return on new capital, a moat, a founder or manager who allocates well (Bezos P1, Huang P3). Numbers: revenue growth, gross margin, free cash flow margin, return on invested capital, share count, and a reverse DCF that shows the growth the price needs. Size: small to medium; the bad case is a halving or worse. Sell: the growth driver slows, the reverse test shows the price needs more than your bull case, or a better idea needs the money. Mistakes: paying for a story at any price, ignoring dilution from stock pay, and confusing a falling price with a falling business.

### Dividend stocks

Check: the dividend is covered by free cash flow, not by earnings alone; debt is modest; the business holds its position. Numbers: payout of free cash flow, interest cover, dividend growth over ten years, total return, and tax on the payout. Size: part of an income plan, never the whole. Sell: a cut is likely (payout above free cash flow, falling revenue), or the yield is high only because the price fell. Mistakes: chasing yield into a failing firm, ignoring that a dividend leaves the company and the tax follows it, and holding one sector.

### Index funds and ETFs

Check: low cost, wide, and simple (Buffett, 2013). Numbers: cost ratio, tracking gap, tax treatment, the mix of the fund (stocks and bonds), and your horizon. Size: the core of the plan for anyone with no edge. Buy on a schedule and in parts; never time the entry. Sell: only to meet the plan's goal, to rebalance, or to pay for a need. Mistakes: selling in a crash, adding a second fund that holds the same thing, paying for a fund that charges more than the index, and treating a thematic ETF as a diversified one.

### Macro and trading

Check: a view on liquidity, rates, flows or a catalyst, and a way to be wrong cheaply (Druckenmiller P3, P4). Numbers: expected gain against loss, the stop level, the position's loss in money, costs and spreads, and a log of every trade. Size: loss budget set below the usual idea size; press only after profit and conviction (Druckenmiller P2: preserve capital, then press). Sell: the stop, the broken thesis, or the target. Mistakes: leverage that makes a right call a loss, no stop, averaging down, trading from boredom, and no log.

### Private equity and venture capital

Check: market size and risk layers (Andreessen P1, P4), founders, the cap table, the price, and the path to a sale or listing. Numbers: ownership after dilution, the return needed to be worth the fund's lock-up, and a 10x gain on invested capital in four to six years (Andreessen P2). Returns come from a few outliers (Thiel P1, Leone P5). Size: many small cheques, with money held back for follow-ons, and no cash you need. Sell: when a buyer offers a price above your hold case, or by a founder's 10-20% partial sale (Leone P1). Mistakes: too few shots, chasing a hot round, ignoring dilution and fees, and pricing by the last round (Leone calls protecting the last round's price a mistake).

### Real estate

Check: location, the tenant market, the condition, the rules (rent control, tax, costs), and the exit. Numbers: net operating income divided by price (cap rate), cash-on-cash return, debt service cover, repairs and vacancy reserves, tax and fees both ways, and the IRR with a sale at the end. Size: one property is a large, illiquid bet; hold a cash reserve for repairs and empty months. Funding: a fixed rate and a payment that survives a year of no rent. Sell: the thesis broke, a better use of the equity exists, or the area changes. Mistakes: counting rent and ignoring costs, a floating rate, no reserve, and buying with emotion.

### Crypto

Check: what gives it value (use, scarcity, a network), who holds it, how you hold it, and what you can lose entirely. Numbers: it has no cash flow, so no DCF. Use size, a stop, and the real cost of custody and fees. Treat it as a power-law bet with a capped loss (Altman P3: lose 1x if wrong, win 100x if right; Thiel P1). Size: an amount you can lose completely. Sell: write the exit plan first: the price, the date or the fact that sells it. Mistakes: borrowing to buy, trusting an exchange with your whole stake, buying because the price rose, and no plan to take profit.

### A business or asset you buy to rent out (for example GPUs)

Check: demand, who pays, what supply is coming, and what replaces the asset. Numbers: monthly revenue = hours in the month × utilisation × price per GPU-hour × (1 − platform fee). Costs: power (kilowatts × hours × price per kilowatt-hour, with cooling), hosting, internet, repairs, financing, tax. Net cash = revenue − costs. Payback = all-in price / net cash per month. Break-even utilisation = fixed monthly cost / (hours × price × (1 − fee)). Depreciation: the resale value falls as new generations arrive and supply grows, so test a lower price per GPU-hour and a lower utilisation. Default (ours): the base case pays back within half the expected useful life, and the stress case still pays back within the full life. Compare with renting the same capacity yourself: if you use it on your own work, its value is the rent you avoid. Size: no more than you can lose if the price per hour halves. Sell: when the price per hour falls under the cost of power and hosting plus a margin, or when the payback case fails. Mistakes: assuming full utilisation, ignoring power and downtime, and buying at the top of a shortage.

### Reselling goods

Check: where you buy below where you sell, and who buys. Numbers: buy price + fees + shipping + returns + your hours = cost; margin = sale − cost; days to sell; turns per year. Annual return = margin per cycle × cycles per year. Size: stock you can sell within a set number of days; limit capital in any one item. Sell: set a markdown date when you buy. Mistakes: counting no time cost, stock that sits, no returns allowance, and a single platform that can close your account.

### Cars

Check: for personal use, a car is spending, not an investment: it usually loses value. As a trade or collector buy, check the price against recent sales, the condition and history, and the market for that model. Numbers: buy price + inspection + repairs + registration and tax + insurance + storage + holding days; compare with a realistic sale price after fees. Margin of safety = (sale price − all costs − buy price) / buy price. Size: small; each car is illiquid. Sell: a set holding limit or target. Mistakes: loving the car, hidden repairs, paying retail and hoping to sell at retail, and loans on an asset that drops.

### Buying a business

Check: what the business sells, why customers stay, how much depends on the owner, customer concentration, and why the owner is selling. The best case, in Buffett's words, is an outstanding business with outstanding management (Buffett P2). Ackman says a great business with a weak team, switched for a great one, creates a lot of value (Ackman, Graham & Doddsville, 2015). Numbers: owner earnings and the price paid as a multiple of them (the years of payback), debt service cover, the working capital the business needs, and a check of the books and the customers. Size: the business is a large, illiquid bet; keep reserves, and borrow only an amount the owner's earnings cover under stress. Sell: usually hold; sell if the thesis breaks or a buyer pays more than the hold case. Mistakes: paying for a growth plan, trusting the seller's numbers without checking them, no key-person plan, and too much debt.
